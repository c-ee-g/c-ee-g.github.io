How it decides

US copyright for a published book depends on when it came out. The year you enter picks the rule:

Renewed means a renewal matching the title and author was filed for a registration in the year you gave (±1). Registered, no renewal found means the book's original registration is in the catalog and no renewal was filed against that registration number. That is the strongest evidence these records can give.

Limits. Transcriptions contain typos, so a near-miss title can hide a renewal: always try a shorter title and the author's surname alone. Books first published outside the US may have had copyright restored in 1996 even without renewal. Illustrations, forewords and revised editions can carry their own copyright. This tool is research help, not legal advice.

Where the records come from

  • Renewals: NYPL CCE renewals dataset (Catalog of Copyright Entries 1950–1977 transcribed by Project Gutenberg volunteers, plus the Copyright Office's own database for 1978–1991).
  • Registrations: NYPL Catalog of Copyright Entries project, book volumes 1931–1963.
  • Stanford's renewal database covers the same 1950–1992 book renewals, but has no registrations, so it can't show that a book was registered and then left unrenewed.
